    Current sensing and current limit¶

    A_ISENSE and B_ISENSE output 0.675 V for every amp flowing in that motor. Read them with an ADC to measure load or detect a stall. On a Raspberry Pi, use a PinGoblin ADS1115 board; an ESP32 or Pico can use its own ADC.

    \[ I_{motor} = \frac{V_{ISENSE}}{0.675\ \mathrm{V/A}} \]
    • The reading is only valid while the motor is driven (EN high). During PWM off-time it drops to zero, so average several samples, or sample in the PWM on-time. The library does this for you (see Current sensing in the API).
    • A stalled motor shows as a reading that sits at the current limit while the shaft isn't turning. A typical approach: if the reading stays above about 80% of the limit for more than 200 ms, stop the motor. The library's stall detection works this way.
    • Each driver limits motor current on every PWM cycle (cycle-by-cycle chopping). The limit is set from VLOGIC:
    Host logic (VLOGIC) Current limit per motor ISENSE at the limit
    3.3 V About 2.0 A About 1.32 V
    5.0 V About 3.0 A About 2.0 V
    • While limiting, the red fault LED lights and nFAULT goes low. Normal operation resumes automatically when the load drops.
    • A short circuit or overheating shuts the driver down. It retries automatically once the fault clears.
    • Both motors share the 3 A board fuse. If both run near their limit at 5 V logic for more than a few seconds, the fuse will trip. Once power is removed it resets within about a minute.

    Is ISENSE related to motor speed?¶

    Not directly. ISENSE measures current, and in a brushed DC motor current follows the load torque, not the speed. At a fixed supply voltage and PWM duty, current and speed are linearly related but in opposite directions: as load slows the motor, its back-EMF falls and the current rises.

    \[ \omega = \frac{D \cdot V_{M} - I \cdot R}{K_e} \]

    Here \(\omega\) is motor speed, \(D\) the PWM duty (0–1), \(V_M\) the motor supply voltage, \(I\) the motor current (\(V_{ISENSE} / 0.675\)), \(R\) the motor's winding resistance and \(K_e\) its back-EMF constant.

    What this means in practice:

    • Fixed duty and supply: speed and current fall on a straight line. Speed is highest at the no-load current and zero at the stall current.
    • Change the duty or the supply: the line shifts, so the same current can mean very different speeds. ISENSE alone can't tell you RPM.
    • No load: a free-running motor draws almost the same small current at any speed, because only friction loads it. ISENSE barely changes with RPM.

    For a rough speed estimate at one fixed duty, calibrate two points. Measure the no-load speed \(\omega_0\) and current \(I_0\), then the stall current \(I_{stall}\) (hold the shaft for under a second). Then:

    \[ \omega \approx \omega_0 \cdot \frac{I_{stall} - I}{I_{stall} - I_0} \]

    Limits of this estimate:

    • The current limit (2 A or 3 A) clamps the current, so the line ends there. Any load heavier than that just reads as the limit.
    • Winding resistance rises about 0.4% per °C as the motor warms, which shifts the line.
    • Gearbox friction and brush noise add error. Average several readings.

    For real speed measurement or closed-loop speed control, use a motor with an encoder (many hobby gearmotors have a Hall encoder) and read it on the host. Use ISENSE for load and stall detection.
