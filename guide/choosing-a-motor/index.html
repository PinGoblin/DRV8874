    Choosing a motor¶

    The board suits brushed DC motors that draw no more than about 2.5 A continuously in normal use. Each motor is limited to 4.6 A (peak), and the board is rated for 5 A continuous, both motors combined. A motor that needs more current than this still runs, but only with reduced torque.

    Check two figures on the motor's datasheet before buying: its stall current and its rated voltage.

    Current¶

    Stall current at the voltage used Result on this board
    Up to 4.2 A Full performance. The current limit never acts, even at stall
    4.2–9 A Good. The current limit acts only near stall, and the motor's efficient operating range is fully available
    9–35 A Light loads only. The motor reaches its no-load speed, but torque is limited to roughly 13–50 % of its stall torque and the fault LED flickers under load
    Above 35 A, or no-load current above 2.5 A Not suitable. The motor spends most of its time at the current limit, the driver runs hot, and start-up may be reported as a stall

    Where only the no-load current and the stall current are given, estimate the share of stall torque available at the board's limit as:

    \[ \frac{4.6\,\text{A} - I_{no\text{-}load}}{I_{stall} - I_{no\text{-}load}} \]

    Motor manufacturers recommend running continuously at no more than 20–30 % of stall torque, so a motor whose stall current is 6–18 A uses the board well.

    Small plastic gearmotor (robot wheel) 3–10 V About 0.07 A About 0.25 A Full performance
    Slow gearhead motor 12 V 0.03–0.07 A 0.35–1.7 A Full performance
    37 mm metal gearmotor, 24 V 24 V About 0.1 A About 3 A Full performance
    37 mm metal gearmotor, 12 V 12 V About 0.2 A About 5.5 A Good
    130-size hobby motor 1.5–4.5 V About 0.25 A Up to about 3.5 A Full performance. Set motor_voltage (see below)
    280/300-size motor 6 V About 0.55 A About 5.6 A Good. About 80 % of stall torque available. Set motor_voltage above a 6 V supply
    Large gearhead motor (high torque) 12 V About 0.45 A About 18 A Good at moderate loads (about 25 % of stall torque)
    380/540-size high-speed motor 12 V 0.6–0.8 A 17–24 A Light to moderate loads (about 15–25 % of stall torque)
    550/775-size high-power motor 6–12 V 1.3–4 A 60–110 A Not suitable

    Choose the higher-voltage version of a motor

    Many motors are sold in 12 V and 24 V versions with the same speed and torque. The 24 V version draws half the current, so on this board it delivers twice the torque before the current limit acts. The board accepts supplies up to 30 V for this reason.

    Voltage¶

    A motor can run from a supply above its rated voltage only if the PWM duty is limited, so that the average voltage across it does not exceed its rating. For example, a 6 V motor on a 12 V supply must never run above 50 % duty. At higher duty it overheats and its brushes wear quickly.

    The library does this for you. Give the board its supply voltage and each motor its rated voltage, and 100 % speed is scaled to the safe duty:

    board = MotorBoard(a_en=12, a_ph=5, b_en=13, b_ph=6,
                   supply_voltage=12.0,                 # motor supply on J1/J2
                   a_options=dict(motor_voltage=6.0),   # 100 % speed = 50 % duty
                   b_options=dict(motor_voltage=12.0))  # 100 % speed = 100 % duty

    The test buttons always drive the motor at 100 % duty. Do not use them with a motor rated below the supply voltage.

    Stall detection for small motors¶

    Without a current sensor, the library detects a stall from the driver's current-limit signal (nFAULT). A motor whose stall current is below 4.2 A never reaches the current limit, so a stall is not detected and the motor carries its full stall current until it is stopped. Small gearmotors can overheat within seconds in this condition. To protect them, fit a current sensor and set stall_current (see Current sensing and current limit), or stop the motor in your own code with a time-out or an end-stop switch.

    Large motors and start-up¶

    A motor with a high no-load current, or a heavy load on its shaft, can take longer than the library's default stall time (0.8 s in total) to reach speed at the current limit. If the library reports STALL at every start, set ramp_time for a soft start, or raise start_grace.
